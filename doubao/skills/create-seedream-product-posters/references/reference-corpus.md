## Mechanism clusters

### A. Monumental launch

References 03, 09, 16, 17, and 22 use low- or medium-density systems. Their richness comes from product scale, architecture, reflections, light paths, and disciplined proof rails. Use only one dominant platform or portal motif.

### B. Conversion-rich daily use

References 02, 06, 07, 08, and 10 use 9–12 modules. They combine a large promise with visible usage proof and a strong action dock. Product, result, and CTA form a triangle.

### C. Cultural and beauty premium

References 01, 04, and 18 rely on material and spatial refinement rather than many stickers. Common devices include silk, warm wood, moon gates, partial portraits, fine borders, and restrained icon rails.

### D. High-density campaign

References 11, 12, 15, 19, and 20 use 11–15 modules. They remain readable because details are grouped into three super-containers: headline, hero story/product field, and conversion dock.

### E. Social narrative

References 13, 14, and 21 make the scene itself the proof. Product remains visible, while notes, brush copy, or UI-style annotations explain the emotion. Do not use this route without a clear audience tension or story.

## Density lessons

- A large headline often consumes 12–22% of the canvas but counts as only one module.
- Three coordinated benefit cells count as one rail, not three unrelated modules.
- Dense references use 2–3 container families at most and repeat one motif.
- Product labels receive calm local contrast even when the overall scene is busy.
- Bottom 12–20% is rarely empty: it closes with price/CTA, logistics, trust, menu, or slogan.
- High density is earned by commerce or narrative. Premium products should not be made dense merely to look expensive.

## Routing rule

Choose the closest reference by **buying mechanism**:

- “why it is better” → launch or feature proof;
- “how it fits life” → routine triangle;
- “what result it creates” → portrait/result dual proof;
- “why buy now” → campaign stack;
- “why share it” → social narrative.

Then translate the pattern through the new product’s color, geometry, material, and usage. Never route only because two products share a color.
